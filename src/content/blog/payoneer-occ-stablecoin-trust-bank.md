Payoneer, the cross-border payments company that many freelancers and online sellers use to get paid, is a step closer to running its own stablecoin bank. On 20 November it announced that the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC), the federal regulator for national banks, had given preliminary conditional approval to PAYO Digital Bank. This week its proposed CEO explained to Payments Dive why Payoneer wants to issue its own token instead of relying on someone else's. The real question for customers is how their money would move once the bank opens, and the charter is only the first step toward that.

## What happened

Payoneer's release, published at 5:02 p.m. Eastern on 20 November, says PAYO Digital Bank has preliminary conditional approval to form as a national trust bank "to support stablecoin functionality for global businesses." The bank is now in what Payoneer calls its organizational phase. It still needs final OCC approval to open and to issue payment stablecoins.

Once it gets that approval, the bank plans to issue PAYO-USD. Payoneer describes it as a fully reserved stablecoin designed to comply with the GENIUS Act, the US federal stablecoin law. PAYO-USD will not be listed on exchanges. It is meant to work as a balance inside the Payoneer ecosystem. According to the release, customers would hold it in bank-approved digital wallets, businesses could pay or get paid in a range of third-party stablecoins, and balances could be converted into local currency in the markets where customers operate. Payoneer says its payout network covers more than 190 markets.

On 24 November, Payments Dive (first published by Banking Dive) added detail from Rob Morgan, PAYO Digital's proposed CEO. The bank will hold the reserves backing PAYO-USD and, Morgan said, can use them to fund payouts in customers' local markets. Issuing its own token lets Payoneer "move customers into that Payoneer-issued balance in real time," he said. He also said about a third of Payoneer's customers already use stablecoins. The bank is still building its platform and working toward its opening exam. Payoneer applied in February, so the approval took about nine months.

There is also a takeover in progress. Nuvei, the Canadian payments processor, said in June it would buy Payoneer for $2.75 billion, and Payoneer is expected to become a Nuvei subsidiary by mid-2027. Morgan declined to say how the deal would affect PAYO Digital, and noted that the two companies currently operate independently.

## Why it matters

The first thing to get right is what this charter is not. When Payoneer applied, Banking Dive reported that the charter would not let it take deposits or make loans. PAYO Digital is a trust bank, so it does not offer insured checking accounts. Its job is narrow: issue a token, hold the reserves behind it, keep custody of those balances, and turn them into fiat when someone needs cash.

That narrow job sits at the centre of Payoneer's business. Today a payment to a freelancer in one country from a marketplace in another passes through several intermediaries: the payer's bank, Payoneer's partner banks, correspondent banks (banks that hold accounts with each other so money can cross borders), and a local payout partner. Each one adds fees, cut-off times and a chance for the payment to get stuck. If the balance in the middle is a token Payoneer issues and backs itself, more of that chain becomes Payoneer's own ledger, available at any hour.

It also changes who earns the float. Reserves behind a fully backed stablecoin are usually held in cash and short-term government debt, and the issuer keeps the interest. Using USDC means Circle holds those reserves. With PAYO-USD, Payoneer's own bank holds them. Neither the release nor Payments Dive gives revenue figures, so I won't guess, but the incentive is obvious.

Payoneer has company in this. Banking Dive listed Circle, Ripple, Paxos, BitGo and Fidelity Digital Assets as receiving conditional OCC approval for national trust charters in December, with Crypto.com and Stripe's Bridge also approved. The OCC charter is turning into the standard licence for US stablecoin infrastructure.

## How "preliminary conditional" approval works

A national bank charter usually comes in two stages, and Payoneer is in the first one.

1. **Preliminary conditional approval.** The OCC agrees in principle that the organisers may form the bank, provided they meet conditions. Payoneer's release doesn't list those conditions. In this phase the bank can hire, build systems and raise capital, but it cannot serve customers or issue PAYO-USD.
2. **Pre-opening work.** The bank builds its platform, controls and staffing, and then has an opening exam, in which the regulator checks that what was promised actually exists. Payments Dive says PAYO Digital is working toward that exam now.
3. **Final approval.** Only after this can the bank open and, per the release, start issuing payment stablecoins.

So "approved" in the headlines means "allowed to build." Some reports have attached specific capital and deadline figures to the approval. I couldn't confirm those from a primary source, so I've left them out.

## My take

I think the strategy makes sense, and the closed-loop design is the most honest part of it. Payoneer isn't trying to compete with USDC on exchanges. It wants a regulated, internal unit of account that it controls from end to end, plus the ability to accept other stablecoins at the edges. For a payouts business, that is a sensible place to draw the line.

I'm less convinced the customer benefit will be as big as the infrastructure change. A freelancer in Manila or Lagos doesn't care what the middle balance is. They care about the exchange rate, the fee and how quickly money arrives in a local account. The last step, converting PAYO-USD into local currency, still depends on local banks and local rules. Stablecoins speed up the middle of a payment. The two ends move at the speed of the slowest bank involved.

The Nuvei question is real, and I wouldn't wave it away. The bank is being built by a company that expects to change hands by mid-2027. Regulators approved the plan of the organisers in front of them, and a new parent with its own priorities could change that plan. Morgan declining to comment is reasonable at this stage, but builders should treat the long-term ownership of the bank as unknown.

Finally, a ledger that runs all the time also fails all the time, at 3 a.m. on Sundays as well as during office hours. In the agent systems I build, the hardest bugs come from ambiguous outcomes, where a request was sent and you don't know if it landed. Payouts have the same problem, which is why my [research on safe retries after ambiguous timeouts](/research/safe-retry-ambiguous-tool-timeout) applies here more than you might expect.

## What to do about it

**For builders:** If you pay out through Payoneer or a similar platform, design for a hybrid period. Some recipients will be paid in stablecoins and some in fiat. Model them as separate rails with their own settlement states, and make every payout idempotent (safe to retry without paying twice). If you're choosing a banking-as-a-service partner, ask whether it plans its own charter or token, and what happens to your integration if it changes owner.

**For teams and companies:** If you pay contractors abroad, ask your provider plainly who holds the reserves behind any stablecoin balance, what the redemption terms are, and whether the balance is insured. On a trust-bank model, assume it is not a deposit.

**For freelancers and sellers:** Nothing changes yet. PAYO-USD doesn't exist until the bank gets final approval. When it arrives, compare the full cost: conversion rate, withdrawal fee and time to reach your local bank. Don't judge it by the label.

## What to watch next

- Whether PAYO Digital passes its opening exam and gets final OCC approval, and whether the conditions are made public.
- Any statement from Nuvei or Payoneer on the bank's role after the acquisition, which is expected to close by mid-2027.
- Which third-party stablecoins PAYO Digital accepts at launch, and in which payout markets local-currency conversion goes live first.
- Whether other cross-border payout firms follow with trust charters of their own, or choose to partner with existing issuers.
